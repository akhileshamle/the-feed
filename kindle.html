Saturday: the 37% rule

For a one-way choice (you can't go back), look at the first 37% of options without picking any. Then take the first option that beats everything you saw in that window. This gives roughly a 37% chance of landing the single best option, far better than random.

Problem: You are viewing 19 flats in one day. Each owner wants a yes or no on the spot, and a rejected flat is gone. You score each flat out of 100. The first ones you see score:

62, 71, 58, 80, 66, 74, 69

After that, the flats come in this order: 68, 77, 79, 84, 90, 73, ...

  1. How many flats do you view without committing?
  2. Which flat (by viewing number) do you take, and what is its score?

Try it first

  • Multiply 19 by 0.37 and round to a whole flat.
  • The observation phase sets your bar: find the best score in it.
  • After that, ignore anything that doesn't beat the bar, and stop at the first that does.

Why it matters: Hiring, house hunting, and picking a parking spot all have this shape. A fixed rule for when to stop looking beats deciding by gut feel as you go.

Answer

  1. 0.37 × 19 = 7.03, so observe the first 7 flats.
  2. The best of those 7 is 80. Then:
  • Flat 8 (68): below 80, skip.
  • Flat 9 (77): below 80, skip.
  • Flat 10 (79): below 80, skip.
  • Flat 11 (84): beats 80, take it.

You take flat 11, scoring 84. The 90 at flat 12 is never seen, which is the cost of the rule: it only gives about a 37% chance of the very best.

Saturday: Entropy and Stability (dS ≥ 0; d²U/dx² > 0)

The idea: Left alone, order decays: files clutter, code rots, budgets drift. Keeping things orderly costs energy, which is maintenance. A system is stable when a small push makes it roll back (like a ball in a bowl, d²U/dx² > 0) and fragile when a small push sends it away (a ball on a hill).

Question: Your weekly routine works only if nothing goes wrong: one missed workout or late night and it collapses. Is it in a bowl or on a hill, and what single change would make it self-correcting?

Application (software engineering): A codebase with no tests or dependency updates drifts toward disorder. Each untouched month adds hidden breakage. A scheduled weekly 30-minute maintenance slot (update dependencies, delete dead code, fix a flaky test) is the energy you spend to hold entropy back. Even better, a CI check that fails loudly when something breaks works as a bowl: deviations get pushed back automatically.

2-minute exercise: Pick one routine you rely on (sleep, finances, inbox, a project). Write:

  1. What is the "small push" that most often disturbs it?
  2. Does it recover on its own, or does it need you to notice?
  3. One restoring force to add (a default, a reminder, a buffer).

Log it: date, concept, my answer, what I applied.

Answer

A routine that collapses after one miss is on a hill: the missed day makes the next miss more likely, so deviation grows rather than shrinks. To convert it to a bowl, add a restoring rule, such as "never miss twice" or a minimum version (10 minutes instead of 60). A miss then pulls you back toward the routine instead of away from it. The cost is the small ongoing effort of keeping that rule, which is the energy that holds entropy back.

Saturday: Mobile System Design (Offline Outbox and Sync)

Why this matters: Offline writes that get lost, duplicated or silently overwritten destroy user trust faster than any crash. At scale, the retry path runs millions of times a day, so every small ambiguity turns into a support ticket.

Scenario: You own a field-inspection app. Technicians edit checklists and attach 5–15 MB photos in basements with no signal, sometimes for hours. The backend is a REST API with last-write-wins semantics. You can add headers (ETag/If-Match, Idempotency-Key) but not a sync protocol. Two technicians sometimes edit the same inspection. The app is often killed or backgrounded before it finishes uploading.

Recall first:

  1. How do you guarantee each edit is applied exactly once despite retries and app termination?
  2. How do you stop one technician from silently overwriting the other's changes when the server is last-write-wins?
  3. What would you do and why: how do you upload the photos and order them against the record edits that reference them?

Answer

Decision: Use a persistent outbox in SQLite, written in the same transaction as the local change. Each entry is a field-level patch with an idempotency key and the ETag it was based on. Entries drain serially per entity and in parallel across entities.

  • Photos: Upload each photo first using a background URLSession upload from a file, which survives termination. The checklist patch that references the photo depends on that upload and is unblocked only when the server returns the asset ID.
  • Conflicts: Send the base ETag in If-Match. On a 412, fetch the server version and rebase the field patches. If the same field changed on both sides, show the conflict to the user.

Rejected:

  • Sending the whole object (PUT) overwrites the other technician's fields.
  • Relying on BGProcessingTask alone fails because it isn't guaranteed to run.
  • CRDTs would need a server you can't change.

Main risk: A poisoned entry at the head of the queue blocks that entity forever. Add capped backoff and a dead-letter state that is visible in the UI.

Common mistake: Generating the idempotency key at send time. Retries then get new keys and create duplicates. Generate it when the entry is enqueued.

struct OutboxOp: Codable {
    let id: UUID          // idempotency key, fixed at enqueue
    let entityID: String
    let baseETag: String?
    let patch: [String: JSONValue]
    var dependsOn: [UUID] // e.g. photo uploads
}
